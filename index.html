The Commoditization of Intelligence

AI Economics Platform Strategy Market Structure

The Concept

Yesterday we talked about what AI agents can do. Today's question is what they cost — and what happens to markets when the answer becomes "almost nothing."

This week, Apple announced it will open Siri to any competing AI assistant in iOS 27 — not just OpenAI. Simultaneously, Google's Gemini Flash-Lite dropped to $0.25 per million tokens. Open-source models like Mistral Small 4 now outperform closed systems three to five times their size and cost zero in licensing fees. The pattern is unmistakable: AI model capability is becoming a commodity. The strategic question has shifted from "who has the best model?" to "who controls the platform, the data, and the distribution?"

The Analogy

Think of electricity in the early 20th century. For two decades, generating your own power was a competitive advantage — industrial companies built private plants to power their factories. Then the grid arrived. Once electricity became cheap, reliable infrastructure, the advantage shifted entirely to what you plugged into it. Nobody wins today by generating better electricity. AI model intelligence is following the same arc. The winners won't be the ones with the best model. They'll be the ones who built the best thing on top of a ubiquitous, cheap, powerful grid.

How to Speak to It — Executive Mode

The naive take is "AI is getting cheaper, great." The sophisticated take reframes the whole competitive map:

"The model wars are effectively over — not because one lab won, but because the underlying intelligence is becoming infrastructure. Apple just proved the point: they're opening Siri to every AI model on the market. When a platform player treats models as interchangeable inputs, you know commoditization has arrived. The question for every company now is: what layer do you own? Because it isn't the model."

"The analogy I keep coming back to is the database wars of the 90s. Oracle, IBM, Sybase — all fighting over who had the best relational engine. Fast forward: the engine became a commodity, and the winners were the companies that owned the applications sitting on top of it. We're watching the same movie with AI, just at faster speed."

Peripheral & Opposing Arguments

The counter: Commoditization isn't uniform. Frontier model capability — the top 1% of reasoning and scientific research — may never commoditize, because the compute requirements are economically brutal. OpenAI just shut down Sora's public API because one minute of video cost more to generate than users would pay. The frontier is pulling ahead faster than the commodity tier catches up.

The geopolitical wrinkle: Chinese models (MiniMax M2.5, Qwen 3.5) are now matching US frontier models at a fraction of the cost, and they're open-source. The commoditization pressure isn't just technological — it's geopolitical. The US "tech decoupling" narrative is complicated by the fact that US startups are quietly building on Chinese open-source infrastructure.

The platform power argument: Apple opening Siri to all models looks like AI democratization. It's actually a power consolidation move: Apple keeps the relationship with 1.5 billion iPhone users while models compete to be selected. The platform wins by making the thing below it a commodity. Classic move. Seen it before with app stores.

Sound Like an Expert — Framing Toolkit

  • "The layer question" — In any platform transition, winners own a layer. Identify which layer before you place strategic bets.
  • "Commoditization pressure" — A more precise term than "competition." It implies structural price erosion, not just rivalry.
  • "Distribution moat" — What Apple, Microsoft, and Google have that model labs don't. A billion relationships built before AI existed.
  • Drop in: "The last company to win by having the best model was probably Google in 2023. After that, the game moved to distribution, data, and trust."
02 · Book of the Day

The Innovator's Dilemma

Clayton Christensen · 1997 · Business Strategy / Technology Legendary

Why This Book, Why Today

No book in business history has better predicted the structural dynamics of today's AI market. Christensen's central insight — that the companies most likely to be disrupted are the well-managed ones, precisely because they listen too closely to their best customers — explains why OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic may be simultaneously dominating and building their own coffins. Today's tech concept (commoditization) is Christensen's disruption theory playing out in real time.

Core Ideas You Can Reapply

Sustaining vs. disruptive innovation. Sustaining innovations make existing products better for existing customers — GPT-5.4 beating GPT-5.3. Disruptive innovations start as "good enough, but much cheaper" at the low end — Mistral Small 4 running on a single GPU. Incumbents always win the sustaining battle. They almost always lose the disruptive one, because they rationally ignore a market that is initially small, low-margin, and populated by customers they don't want.

The resource allocation problem. Disruption doesn't fail because of bad strategy. It fails because large organizations allocate resources toward what their existing customers are paying for. The market's signals are rational — and they're the wrong signals. Every dollar flowing to GPT-5.4 "Pro" features is a dollar not flowing to the $0.25/million-token model that will own the mass market in three years.

The strategic implication: If you're a large enterprise building an AI strategy right now, you're at risk of the same failure mode — over-investing in frontier AI capabilities for your power users while the commodity model quietly becomes "good enough" for 90% of your workforce's actual needs. The disruption happens below your line of sight.

"The question Christensen would ask about your AI strategy is not 'what's the best model for your best use case?' It's 'what's the cheapest model that's good enough for your most common use case, and are you building on top of that?'"

Quote-Worthy Phrases

"The reason why it is so difficult for existing firms to capitalize on disruptive innovations is that their processes and values constitute disabilities when confronted with change."
"Customers, not technology, drive disruption. The question is always: which customers are you not yet serving?"

In a Debate Setting

Rebuttal to "the big labs will always win": Christensen would say the opposite — well-managed incumbents are the most vulnerable to disruption precisely because their rational decision-making optimizes for their current customers. The companies that build on cheap, open-source AI for under-served markets are the ones to watch.

Peripheral support: Andy Grove's "Only the Paranoid Survive" extends this — Grove called the moment where disruption arrives a "strategic inflection point," and argued the only way to see it coming is to listen to the people on the edges of your organization, not the center. Applied to AI: your frontline teams using cheap AI tools are the leading indicators, not your AI strategy committee.

💬 Critical Thinking Prompt

Prompt: Christensen argued that incumbents fail not from incompetence but from rational management. Apply this framework: which current AI-first company is most at risk of being disrupted by a cheaper, "good enough" competitor — and what is the rational management decision that will cause it?

Example answer (form your own first):

OpenAI is the most interesting candidate. Their rational management decision is to charge premium prices for frontier models to serve the enterprise and developer customers who are their current revenue base. That's a completely sound decision — those customers pay, demand quality, and validate the product. The disruption risk: open-source models (Mistral, Qwen) are approaching "good enough" for 70-80% of enterprise use cases at near-zero cost. The customers OpenAI can't profitably serve — budget-constrained startups, emerging market developers, high-volume low-margin applications — will build entirely on open-source. That ecosystem will compound. Five years from now, OpenAI may find that the mass market learned AI development without them, and the only customers left are the ones who need the absolute frontier. Which is a fine, high-margin niche — unless a Chinese lab closes that gap too.

03 · Daily Comm Pack

Framework + Word Bank

3A · Framework: The SCQA Structure

Source: Barbara Minto, The Pyramid Principle — the foundational McKinsey communication framework. Builds on PREP from Day 1 by adding narrative tension before the answer.

What it is: SCQA creates a mini-story arc that pulls the listener forward before you deliver your answer. Where PREP leads with the point, SCQA earns the point by first establishing why it matters. Use SCQA for written communication, presentations, and any setting where you need to frame a problem before solving it.

S — Situation
Establish shared ground State what everyone already knows and agrees on. Keep it brief — this is not your argument, it's the runway.
C — Complication
Introduce the tension What has changed, gone wrong, or created a new challenge? This is the "but" that makes the situation unstable. The sharper the complication, the more your audience needs your answer.
Q — Question
Surface the implicit question The question the audience is now asking in their heads. You don't always say this aloud — but knowing it forces you to answer the right thing.
A — Answer
Lead with your recommendation Now deliver the answer. Because you built the tension, the answer lands with weight. This is where PREP takes over — your answer, your reasons, your example.

Applied to today's topic — opening a board presentation on AI strategy:

[S] For the past two years, our AI strategy has centered on selecting the best frontier model for each use case.

[C] This month, Apple opened its platform to every AI model on the market, open-source models matched frontier performance at near-zero cost, and inference prices dropped 80% year-over-year. The model selection game has fundamentally changed.

[Q] (implied) So where should we actually be placing our bets?

[A] We should stop competing on model selection and start competing on data and distribution — the two layers that commoditization cannot erode.

Day 1 vs. Day 2 framework: Use PREP for impromptu answers and Q&A. Use SCQA for structured presentations, memos, and opening a conversation you're controlling. Together they cover 80% of your executive communication needs.

Agentic AI:
From Chatbot to Coworker

AI Agents Enterprise AI Future of Work

The Concept

For three years, AI was a conversationalist — you asked, it answered, it stopped. Agentic AI breaks that pattern. An AI agent doesn't wait for your next prompt. It receives a goal, breaks it into steps, selects tools, executes, checks its own output, course-corrects, and delivers a result — often across multiple software systems — without hand-holding.

This week, GPT-5.4 crossed a threshold: it scored 75% on OSWorld-V, a benchmark that simulates real desktop tasks. The human baseline is 72.4%. That is the first time an AI system has matched white-collar knowledge work in a controlled test. Jensen Huang declared OpenClaw — the open-source agentic framework that lets AI agents operate through iMessage, Slack, or WhatsApp — the "next ChatGPT." He called it the agentic operating system the industry has been waiting for.

The Analogy

Think of previous AI as a brilliant intern who answers every question perfectly — but sits idle until you come back with the next one. Agentic AI is the transition from intern to autonomous contractor: you hand them a project brief on Monday, and they email you the deliverable on Friday, having made hundreds of micro-decisions along the way. The question is no longer "can it answer?" It's "can I trust it to act?"

How to Speak to It — Executive Mode

When this comes up in a board room or podcast, don't lead with the technology. Lead with the organizational implication:

"We are not in the chatbot era anymore. We're in the era of the digital workforce. The question every executive should be asking right now is not 'how do we use AI?' — it's 'what does our org chart look like when AI handles the routine cognitive layer?' The companies that answer that question first will have a structural cost advantage that compounds every quarter."

Layer in the specific data point when pressed:

"GPT-5.4 just matched human performance on real desktop knowledge work. That's not a marketing benchmark — that's the threshold moment. We can now, for the first time, credibly say some classes of white-collar work are automatable at scale."

Peripheral & Opposing Arguments — For Critical Thinking

The bull case (lean into this): Every major technology platform — PC, internet, mobile — created more jobs than it destroyed at the macro level. AI agents are likely the next productivity multiplier, not the final one.

The bear case (steelman this): Previous platforms augmented physical or distributional capabilities. Agentic AI directly targets cognitive labor — the category humans thought was permanently safe. The economic disruption could be faster, more concentrated, and less easily reabsorbed.

The governance gap (the sophisticated angle): NVIDIA's NemoClaw enterprise stack ships a "privacy router" that monitors all agent outgoing communications. That tells you something: even the optimists building this technology know that trust infrastructure for agents doesn't exist yet. We're deploying autonomous digital coworkers without the audit trails, liability frameworks, or institutional norms that govern human coworkers. That is the underappreciated risk.

Sound Like an Expert — Framing Toolkit

  • "The shift from tool to actor" — AI going from something you use to something that acts on your behalf.
  • "Trust infrastructure" — the governance, audit, and accountability layer that agentic AI currently lacks.
  • "Cognitive labor arbitrage" — the economic mechanism by which agent AI will reprice knowledge work.
  • Drop in: "The benchmark that matters isn't Turing. It's whether the agent can book my flight and update my CRM without me checking its work."
02 · Book of the Day

Thinking, Fast and Slow

Daniel Kahneman · 2011 · Psychology / Decision-Making Legendary

Why This Book, Why Today

Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics for proving that humans are not rational actors — we are fast, pattern-matching, narrative-seeking animals who occasionally do slow, deliberate reasoning. In an era where AI agents make decisions faster than any human can supervise, understanding how humans actually think is the prerequisite to understanding what we're actually delegating — and what we're incapable of supervising.

Core Ideas You Can Reapply

System 1 vs. System 2. System 1 is fast, intuitive, automatic. System 2 is slow, effortful, deliberate. Most of our lives run on System 1. The problem: System 1 is riddled with cognitive biases — availability heuristic, anchoring, the halo effect. We mistake fluency for accuracy, familiarity for truth.

The practical application: Every high-stakes decision you make in business — M&A, hiring, strategy bets — is largely driven by System 1 masquerading as System 2. The executive who knows this builds deliberate friction into decisions: pre-mortems, red teams, mandatory waiting periods.

The AI connection: AI agents are pure System 1 at scale — extraordinarily fast, pattern-matched on human data, but exhibiting the same class of errors. Confident, fluent, occasionally catastrophically wrong. Deploying agentic AI without System 2 oversight is like running an organization entirely on gut instinct.

"The confidence we feel in our judgments is not a reliable signal of their accuracy. Speed of thought and quality of thought are different things — and we spend our careers confusing them." — reframing Kahneman for a business context

Quote-Worthy Phrases

"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is, while you are thinking about it."
"A reliable way to make people believe in falsehoods is frequent repetition, because familiarity is not easily distinguished from truth."

In a Debate Setting

Rebuttal to "AI will make better decisions than humans": AI was trained on human output — which was largely System 1. It inherited our biases at scale, without our occasional System 2 correction. The question is not whether machines are biased. They are. The question is whether their biases are more predictable and correctable than ours.

Peripheral support — for broader arguments: Behavioral economics (Thaler, Sunstein's "nudge theory") built directly on Kahneman's foundation. The design of any system that interacts with humans — pricing, policy, AI interfaces — is fundamentally an exercise in understanding cognitive biases.

💬 Critical Thinking Prompt

Prompt: If human judgment is systematically biased (Kahneman), and AI is trained on the outputs of that biased judgment, what does that mean for the long-term reliability of AI-assisted decision-making in organizations? Where does this logic break down?

Example answer (reference only — form your own first):

The logic holds for AI systems trained purely to replicate human decisions — like early content recommendation algorithms that learned to maximize engagement by amplifying outrage, a bias humans had but couldn't see at scale. It partially breaks down in two places: first, where AI is trained on structured outcomes rather than human opinions — actuarial data, physics simulations — it can correct human bias, not inherit it. Second, AI bias is at least legible and auditable in principle; human bias lives in unconscious heuristics that resist inspection. The honest answer is that we're at the beginning of a long calibration period where we have to learn which decisions to trust to which systems — and that requires epistemic humility we don't culturally practice yet.

03 · Daily Comm Pack

Framework + Word Bank

3A · Framework: The PREP Method

Source: Classic executive communication — widely cited by McKinsey facilitators and executive coaches for impromptu speaking.

What it is: A four-part structure for answering any question on the spot, in a way that sounds structured, confident, and complete. The antidote to rambling.

P
Point — Lead with your answer State your position immediately. Don't build to it. Executives don't have patience for warm-ups.
R
Reason — Why you hold that view One or two crisp reasons. Not five. Five reasons is noise.
E
Example — Make it concrete A data point, an analogy, or a story. This is what makes the abstract land.
P
Point (restated) — Close with authority Return to your answer. Don't trail off. This is what they remember.

Applied to today's topic — if asked in a board meeting: "Should we be investing in agentic AI now?"

[P] Yes — the window for early-mover advantage is narrow and closing fast.

[R] Two reasons: agent benchmarks just crossed human performance thresholds, and the open-source infrastructure to build on is now free.

[E] GPT-5.4 matched human performance on real desktop knowledge work this month. The tools to run agents on your existing stack — Slack, your CRM, your ERP — are available today at zero licensing cost.

[P] Companies that wait for the category to "mature" will be buying in at the price of a proven market. The asymmetric bet is now.
